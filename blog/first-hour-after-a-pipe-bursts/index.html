What to Do in the First Hour After a Pipe Bursts

The order you do these in actually matters. Here's the sequence that limits damage the most.

By the Cary Pro Plumbing team · September 14, 2026

Talk to a plumber
(919) 944-7281
Call Now

A burst pipe is one of the few plumbing situations where what a homeowner does in the first few minutes measurably changes how much damage ends up happening. None of the steps below require a plumber's tools, just knowing what to do before one arrives.

Why This Sequence, Specifically

Repairs performed after a burst pipe follow the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code, but that standard governs the repair itself, not the emergency response. The sequence below is about limiting damage in the minutes before a professional arrives.

1. Shut Off the Main Water Valve

This comes before anything else. The main shutoff valve is usually located where the water line enters the house, often in a basement, crawlspace, or utility closet, or near the water meter outside. Turning it off stops new water from feeding the burst, even though water already in the pipes downstream will still drain out briefly.

2. Shut Off Electricity to the Affected Area, If It's Safe

If water is anywhere near outlets, switches, or a breaker panel, and the panel itself is reachable without stepping into standing water, shut off power to that area. Never touch an electrical panel while standing in water. If reaching it safely isn't possible, leave it and wait for help rather than taking the risk.

3. Start Removing Standing Water

Once the water source is off, remove as much standing water as possible with towels, a mop, or a wet vac if one's available. This isn't about fully drying the area, just reducing how long materials sit saturated before professional extraction equipment arrives.

4. Move What You Can Safely Reach

Furniture, rugs, and anything valuable sitting in or near the affected area should be moved out if it's safe to do so. Anything already soaked can usually still be assessed later; the priority is stopping unaffected items from becoming affected ones.

5. Photograph the Damage Before Cleanup Continues

Take photos of standing water and any visibly affected walls, flooring, or belongings before extraction and drying work fully changes the scene. This matters for an insurance claim regardless of how the burst happened, and it's much harder to document convincingly after the fact.

6. Call for Both the Repair and the Cleanup

A burst pipe is actually two separate jobs: fixing the pipe itself, and drying out whatever the water reached. Water damage restoration handles the extraction and drying, while the plumbing repair stops the source. Mentioning both when you call means the right equipment and the right technician get scheduled the first time, rather than needing a second call once the water's already been dealt with.

Why the First Hour Specifically Matters

Mold can begin developing in wet drywall or carpet padding within 24 to 48 hours of water exposure. The faster standing water is addressed and drying equipment is in place, the more likely materials are to be salvageable rather than needing full replacement. None of the steps above replace a professional response, but they meaningfully change what that response is dealing with by the time it arrives.

What Not to Do

Avoid using a wet vac or any electrical device in standing water without confirming power is off to that area first. Avoid trying to repair or clamp the burst section yourself under pressure, since a temporary fix that fails can cause a second, worse flood. Avoid waiting to see if it's "not that bad" before shutting off the main; the volume of water a burst pipe releases is almost always more than it initially appears.

Why Winter Bursts Are More Common, Even In North Carolina

A hard freeze doesn't need to last long to crack an exposed pipe. Water expands as it freezes, and a section of pipe in an uninsulated crawlspace, exterior wall, or garage is the most common failure point during a cold snap here, even one that only lasts a night or two. Insulating exposed pipe and keeping cabinet doors open near exterior walls during a hard freeze warning reduces this risk, though it doesn't eliminate it entirely.

Common Causes Worth Knowing After the Fact

Freeze damage, corrosion in an aging line, and a sudden pressure spike are the three most common reasons a pipe bursts. Knowing which applies doesn't change the immediate response, but it does matter for the repair conversation afterward, since a corroded line raises the question of whether nearby sections are at similar risk.

What to Expect When Help Arrives

A plumber will locate and repair the actual burst section, which may require accessing it through a wall, ceiling, or floor depending on where it happened. A separate water damage crew, if needed, will extract standing water and set up drying equipment that typically runs for several days, with moisture checks along the way to confirm materials are actually dry before anything gets closed back up or replaced.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find my main water shutoff if I've never used it?

Check near the water meter outside, or in a basement, crawlspace, or utility closet where the main line enters the home. It's worth locating this before an emergency, not during one.

Is a burst pipe always covered by insurance?

Sudden, accidental bursts are typically covered, though coverage details vary by policy. Gradual damage from a long-term leak is often treated differently.

Can I turn the water back on before the repair is finished?

No, leave the main off until the repair is complete and confirmed, even if the immediate flooding has stopped.

Should I call my insurance company before or after calling a plumber?

Either order works, but don't wait on one to start the other. Both calls matter and neither should delay the immediate water shutoff steps above.

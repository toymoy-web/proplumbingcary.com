How to Tell If You Have a Slab Leak

A slab leak rarely announces itself clearly. Here's what actually points to one.

By the Cary Pro Plumbing team · September 14, 2026

Talk to a plumber
(919) 944-7281
Call Now

A slab leak happens when a water line running underneath a concrete foundation develops a crack or pinhole and starts leaking into the ground or up through the slab itself. Unlike a leak under a sink, there's nothing to see at first, which is exactly why so many slab leaks go unnoticed for weeks or months before someone calls a plumber.

The Water Bill Is the First Clue for Most Homeowners

Before any visible sign shows up, most people notice a water bill that's climbed with no real change in household water use. If every fixture is off and the water meter is still turning, water is going somewhere, and a slab leak is one of the most common reasons why.

This is worth checking yourself before calling anyone: shut off every fixture in the house, then watch the meter for a few minutes. Movement with everything off is a strong signal, though it doesn't tell you where the leak is, only that one exists.

A Warm Spot on the Floor Usually Means the Hot Line

If a patch of flooring feels noticeably warmer than the rest of the room, especially over concrete, that's often the hot water line leaking underneath. The warmth comes from hot water escaping into the slab and surrounding soil, and it tends to show up before any actual moisture is visible on the surface.

Sounds That Don't Match Any Running Fixture

A hissing sound coming from the floor when no fixture is in use is a less common but real sign, especially with a higher-pressure leak. It's easy to dismiss as a house settling or a distant appliance, so it's worth paying attention to whether the sound is actually tied to the water system.

What a Slab Leak Is Not

A slow drain, a musty smell from a cabinet, or a spike in humidity are more often tied to a different issue like a clogged drain or a ventilation problem, not a slab leak specifically. Confusing the two can lead to paying for the wrong diagnostic approach, which is part of why an actual leak detection visit, rather than a guess, is worth it once the water bill or a warm spot has raised the question.

How a Slab Leak Actually Gets Confirmed

Electronic leak detection equipment traces the sound and location of water escaping a pressurized line, which pinpoints the leak without cutting into the slab first. That confirmation step matters because slab leak repairs range from a targeted spot repair to rerouting the line entirely, and the right approach depends on exactly where and how bad the leak is, not just that one exists.

If a slab leak has already been running long enough to reach flooring or drywall, that's a separate water damage restoration conversation from the leak repair itself. Fixing the pipe and drying out the resulting damage are two different jobs with two different timelines.

The Bottom Line

A climbing water bill with no clear cause, a warm patch on the floor, or a hissing sound from underneath the slab are the three most reliable signs. None of them confirm a slab leak on their own, but together, or even individually, they're worth an actual diagnostic visit rather than waiting to see if the problem gets more obvious. It generally doesn't; it just gets more expensive.

Before You Call: What to Check First

Shut off the water main only if you suspect active, ongoing loss rather than a slow, long-term leak, since a slab leak usually doesn't require an emergency shutoff the way a burst pipe does. Note the current water meter reading so a technician can compare it against a later reading to confirm the leak rate. Avoid attempting to dig into the slab yourself.

Why Slab Leaks Happen in the First Place

Most slab leaks trace back to one of three causes: corrosion in an aging copper line, a factory defect or poor connection at a joint, or abrasion where a pipe rubs against the concrete or rebar during normal ground shifting over years. None of these are things a homeowner can prevent, which is why the focus is usually on catching a leak early rather than avoiding one entirely.

Does Every Home Have the Same Risk?

Homes with copper supply lines running through or under the slab carry more risk than homes built with PEX, which is more resistant to the kind of pinhole corrosion that causes most slab leaks. Older homes in established neighborhoods are more likely to have copper, while newer construction more often uses PEX, though this varies enough by builder that it's not a reliable assumption on its own.

Repair Standards We Follow

Any repair work follows the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code, regardless of whether the fix is a targeted spot repair or a larger reroute.

What Happens If a Slab Leak Goes Unaddressed

Beyond the water bill, an ongoing slab leak can eventually erode the soil supporting the foundation in that spot, leading to settling or cracking that's far more expensive to address than the original leak. Mold and moisture damage inside walls or flooring above the leak are also common once enough water has migrated upward over time. Catching it at the water-bill-and-warm-spot stage is meaningfully cheaper than catching it at the foundation-settling stage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a slab leak fix itself?

No. A pinhole leak in copper or a cracked joint only gets worse over time as water pressure continues to work at the same weak point.

Will homeowners insurance cover a slab leak?

Coverage varies by policy, but many policies cover resulting damage to the home while excluding the cost of the plumbing repair itself. Check your specific policy or ask your agent.

How long does a slab leak repair take?

A confirmed spot repair is often completed in a single visit once the leak has been located. A full reroute takes longer and is scoped individually.

Does a slab leak always mean the whole foundation is at risk?

Not usually. Caught reasonably early, a slab leak is a localized repair. Foundation risk only becomes a real concern after prolonged, undetected water loss into the soil beneath it.

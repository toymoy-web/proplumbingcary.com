Signs Your Cary Home Needs a Repipe, Not Another Repair

One leak is a repair. A repeated pattern of them is usually something else entirely.

By the Cary Pro Plumbing team · September 19, 2026

Talk to a plumber
(919) 944-7281
Call Now

Every home eventually needs a pipe repaired somewhere. The question worth asking isn't whether a single repair is warranted, it's whether the repairs are starting to form a pattern that points to the pipe material itself rather than one unlucky section of it.

Standards A Repipe Follows

All repiping work follows the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code, including material and support spacing requirements regardless of whether PEX or copper is used.

Repeated Leaks in Unrelated Locations

A leak under a bathroom sink and, eight months later, a leak in an upstairs wall are unrelated locations with, potentially, the same underlying cause: aging pipe material failing in multiple spots as it reaches the end of its service life. One leak is normal wear. Two or three unrelated ones within a couple of years is a pattern worth paying attention to.

Discolored Water From Multiple Fixtures

Rust-colored or discolored water from more than one fixture, not just the one closest to a water heater, suggests corrosion happening inside the supply lines themselves rather than a single failing fixture or connection. This is a stronger signal than discoloration from just one tap, which is more often a localized issue.

Water Pressure Dropping Everywhere, Not Just One Fixture

Pressure loss isolated to one faucet is usually that faucet's own aerator or cartridge. Pressure loss across the entire house, noticeable at multiple fixtures around the same time, more often points to mineral buildup narrowing the pipes throughout the home's supply system.

Polybutylene Pipe, Regardless of Current Symptoms

Homes built with gray polybutylene supply lines, common in some construction between the 1970s and mid-1990s, are past the window where this material is known to fail, whether or not it's shown symptoms yet. This is one of the few cases where a proactive repipe is worth discussing even without an active leak pattern, simply because of the material itself.

What to Do Before You Call

Write down when each past leak happened and roughly where, even if the details feel minor. A technician uses that history, not just the current visible issue, to tell whether a real pattern exists. Avoid patching a visible leak yourself in a way that would hide it from an assessment.

What a Repipe Actually Involves

A whole-home repipe doesn't necessarily mean the whole project happens at once. It's often done in phases, room by room, which keeps the home livable during the work and spreads the cost over time. PEX and copper are the two common material choices, each with real tradeoffs in cost, installation complexity, and long-term durability.

When Repair Still Makes More Sense

A single leak, even a significant one, in an otherwise sound plumbing system is still usually a leak detection and repair situation, not a repiping one. The distinguishing factor is genuinely the pattern, not the severity of any one incident. A whole-home assessment is the most reliable way to tell which situation actually applies before committing to either path.

How a Whole-Home Assessment Actually Works

Rather than inspecting one problem area, a whole-home assessment maps the condition and material of supply lines throughout the house, checking accessible sections in walls, crawlspaces, and utility areas for signs of corrosion, pressure issues, or material type. This gives a full picture rather than extrapolating from a single visible leak.

Cost Considerations Worth Understanding Upfront

A repipe is a larger expense than a single repair, but the comparison that actually matters is against the cumulative cost of repeated repairs plus any water damage cleanup each one causes. Three separate repair-and-cleanup cycles over two years can add up to a significant portion of a repipe's cost, without solving the underlying issue that caused all three.

How Long a Repipe Takes

Timeline depends heavily on whether the project is done all at once or phased. A single-phase repipe on an average home is often completed within a few days, while a phased approach spreads the same work over a longer period to keep portions of the home usable throughout.

How Repiping Affects A Home's Water Pressure Test

Once new lines are installed, the entire system is pressure tested before any wall is closed up for good. This confirms every joint and connection holds under normal operating pressure, not just that water flows, which is a meaningfully different and more thorough check than simply turning the water back on and looking for obvious leaks.

What Happens To Fixtures During A Repipe

Existing faucets, toilets, and other fixtures are typically reconnected to the new supply lines rather than replaced, unless a fixture is already worn enough to warrant replacement anyway. This keeps the project focused on the actual problem, the supply line material, without turning it into a full fixture renovation unless that's specifically part of the plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a repipe disruptive to live through?

It involves some wall access and temporary water shutoffs in the areas being worked on, though phasing can limit how much of the home is affected at once.

Do I need to repipe the whole house, or can I do sections?

Sections are possible, especially if the issue is isolated to one part of the home's plumbing system, though a full assessment is what determines whether that's actually the case.

Will a repipe fix low water pressure?

If the pressure loss is from mineral buildup narrowing old pipes, yes. If it's from a municipal supply issue, a repipe won't change that. A whole-home assessment distinguishes between the two before any work is scoped.

Does a repipe require permits?

In most jurisdictions yes, since it's a significant plumbing change. Permitting is handled as part of the project rather than left to the homeowner.

Can a repipe be done without opening any walls at all?

Rarely completely, though targeted access points minimize how much wall opening is needed compared to a full demolition approach, and every opening is patched as part of the job.

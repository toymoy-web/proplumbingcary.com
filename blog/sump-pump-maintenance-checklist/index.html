Sump Pump Maintenance Checklist for Storm Season

A few minutes of checking now beats finding out it's broken during a storm.

By the Cary Pro Plumbing team · September 14, 2026

Talk to a plumber
(919) 944-7281
Call Now

A sump pump that's been sitting unused since the last time it rained heavily is exactly the kind of thing that fails silently until the moment it's actually needed. A quick seasonal check catches most problems before they matter.

Test the Float Switch

Slowly pour a bucket of water into the sump pit until the float rises enough to trigger the pump. It should switch on, pump the water down, and switch back off cleanly. A float that sticks, either not triggering or not resetting, is one of the most common causes of a pump that fails when it's actually needed.

Check the Discharge Line

Confirm the discharge line outside the home is clear and directing water well away from the foundation, not just a few feet from where it originated. A blocked or improperly routed discharge line can undo all the benefit of a working pump by sending water right back toward the house.

Listen for Unusual Noise During the Test

Grinding, rattling, or a labored sound during the test run can indicate debris in the impeller or a failing motor bearing. A pump that's struggling during a routine test is more likely to fail outright during an actual storm, when it typically runs longer and harder.

Confirm the Backup Power Situation

If the pump has a battery backup, check that the battery is holding a charge, since a battery backup that's dead defeats its entire purpose during the power outages that often accompany serious storms. A standard pump without backup power should be understood as vulnerable during exactly the conditions it's most needed.

Clear the Pit of Debris

Small debris, gravel, or sediment in the sump pit can interfere with the float switch or get pulled into the pump itself. A quick visual check and clearing of any obvious debris takes just a minute and prevents a completely avoidable failure.

When to Call Instead of DIY-ing the Check

If the float sticks, the pump makes concerning noise, or it simply doesn't turn on during the water test, that's the point to call for sump pump repair rather than waiting to see if it resolves on its own. A pump identified as failing during a calm, dry check is a routine repair; the same pump failing during an actual storm is an emergency.

What A Battery Backup Actually Buys You

A battery backup typically runs a pump for several hours during an outage, enough to cover most storm-related outages, though not an extended multi-day one without additional capacity.

A Note On Older Homes

Older homes without a sump pump at all sometimes have a crawlspace or basement history of water intrusion that was simply tolerated for years. It's worth a second look with current storm patterns in mind, even if it's never been a formal complaint before.

What To Do If The Test Fails Right Before A Storm

If a check reveals a failed pump with a storm already forecast, a temporary manual bailing setup or an emergency same-day repair call are both better than waiting, since even a few hours of a working pump during peak rainfall can prevent significant flooding.

Final Thought

A sump pump earns its cost the one time a storm actually tests it, which is exactly why a check when nothing's wrong matters more than it seems.

What A Professional Check Adds Beyond A DIY Test

A professional visit tests under load, inspects the check valve on the discharge line, and confirms the pit itself is properly sized and positioned, catching issues a basic bucket test at home wouldn't reveal.

Cost of Maintenance Versus Cost of a Failure

A seasonal check costs nothing but a few minutes of time. A sump pump failure during an actual storm can mean a flooded crawlspace or basement, water damage cleanup, and potential mold remediation, costs that are entirely avoidable with the basic checks outlined above.

Standards This Work Follows

Sump pump installation and repair follows the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code for discharge routing and backflow prevention on the discharge line, whether the work is routine maintenance or a full replacement.

If You Don't Have a Sump Pump Yet

A crawlspace or low area that's come close to standing water during past heavy rain, even without an actual pump installed, is worth a conversation about installing one proactively rather than waiting for an actual flooding event to force the decision.

Standard This Work Follows

Sump pump installation and repair follows the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code for discharge routing and backflow prevention, whether the work is a routine maintenance visit or a full new installation.

Documenting The Check For Insurance Purposes

Taking a quick photo after a successful test, showing the pump and discharge line in working condition, creates a simple record that can be useful if a future water damage claim ever comes up and documentation of prior maintenance matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should this checklist be done?

Before storm season each year is a reasonable minimum, with an additional check after any extended period of non-use.

Is it normal for a sump pump to run frequently during heavy rain?

Yes, frequent cycling during active heavy rain is expected. Constant running with no rain at all is more concerning and worth having checked.

Do all homes in this area need a sump pump?

Not universally, but any crawlspace or low area with a history of water intrusion during heavy rain is a reasonable candidate.

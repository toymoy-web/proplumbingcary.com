Tank vs Tankless Water Heater: Which Fits Your Cary Home

Both options work well. The right one depends on your household, not which is objectively "better."

By the Cary Pro Plumbing team · September 19, 2026

Talk to a plumber
(919) 944-7281
Call Now

When a water heater fails, the tank-versus-tankless question comes up almost immediately, usually while a homeowner is standing in a cold shower trying to make a decision faster than they'd like to. Here's the honest version of the comparison, without pretending one option is universally right.

How They Actually Work Differently

A tank water heater keeps a set volume of water hot and ready at all times, refilling and reheating as it's used. A tankless unit heats water on demand as it flows through, with no storage tank at all. That single difference explains almost every tradeoff between them.

FactorTankTankless
Upfront costLowerHigher, including possible venting or gas line upgrades
Hot water supplyLimited by tank sizeContinuous, but limited by flow rate at peak demand
Typical lifespan10 to 15 years20 or more years with regular maintenance
MaintenanceOccasional flush for sedimentAnnual descaling recommended, especially with hard water
Space neededLarger footprint, usually a closet or garageCompact, wall-mounted

Installation Standards Apply Either Way

Both tank and tankless installations follow the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code, including venting and clearance requirements specific to each unit type.

Household Size Matters More Than the Marketing Suggests

Tankless units are often marketed as solving hot water shortages, and they do, but only up to their rated flow rate. A large household running two showers and a dishwasher at once needs a tankless unit sized for that load, or it will still run short, just for a different reason than a tank running out. Sizing correctly matters more than the tank-versus-tankless choice itself.

Cary's Water Hardness Affects Tankless Units More

Because a tankless unit heats water as it flows across a heat exchanger, mineral scale from hard water builds up directly on that exchanger, gradually reducing output until a flush clears it. A tank collects sediment at the bottom instead, which affects efficiency and lifespan but shows up differently, usually as popping or rumbling noise rather than reduced output. Neither is a reason to avoid an option entirely, but it does mean a tankless unit in this area benefits more from staying on a regular flush and descale schedule than the same unit would in a softer-water region.

When Repair Still Beats Replacement

Neither option is a reason to replace a unit that's still within a reasonable lifespan and has a single, identifiable fault. A tank under 8 years old with a bad thermostat, or a tankless unit that just needs a flush, is usually still worth repairing rather than replacing outright.

The Practical Answer

If the current setup has been reliable and the household size hasn't changed, matching what's already there is usually the simplest path. If hot water has genuinely been a limiting factor, or the current unit has failed for the second or third time, that's when switching from tank to tankless, or the reverse, is worth seriously considering rather than automatically replacing like for like.

Installation Complexity Is Often the Deciding Factor

Switching from tank to tankless isn't always a simple swap. Tankless units often require different venting, and depending on the unit's output, a larger gas line than what's already installed. This doesn't rule out the switch, but it does mean the real cost comparison has to include any needed infrastructure changes, not just the unit price itself.

Resale Value Considerations

Tankless water heaters are generally viewed as a desirable feature by home buyers, particularly in a market where buyers are comparing operating costs. That said, a well-maintained tank unit with documented service history isn't a red flag either. This is a minor factor compared to household fit, but worth knowing if a future sale is part of the decision.

What Actually Fails on Each Type

Tank units most commonly fail due to sediment buildup affecting the heating element, or the tank itself corroding from the inside after 10 to 15 years. Tankless units more commonly develop scale on the heat exchanger or issues with the flow sensor or igniter, problems that are often resolved with service rather than replacement given the unit's longer expected lifespan.

Warranty Terms Differ Between The Two

Tank units typically carry shorter warranty periods on the tank itself, often 6 to 12 years, reflecting their shorter expected lifespan. Tankless units commonly carry longer warranties on the heat exchanger specifically, often 12 to 15 years, though routine maintenance like annual flushing is frequently a stated condition for keeping that warranty valid.

Fuel Type Changes The Comparison Too

Electric tankless units need significant electrical capacity, sometimes more than an older home's panel can supply without an upgrade, while gas tankless units need adequate gas line sizing instead. Whichever fuel type the home already uses tends to make one option meaningfully easier to install than switching fuel types along with switching tank types.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is tankless actually cheaper to run long term?

Generally yes, due to not maintaining a tank of hot water around the clock, though the savings depend on household hot water use patterns.

Can I install a tankless unit in the same spot as my old tank?

Often yes for the water connections, but venting requirements are different enough that placement sometimes needs to change.

Do tankless units work during a power outage?

Gas tankless units typically need electricity for ignition and controls, so most won't operate during an outage without a backup power source.

Is a bigger tank always better for a large family?

Not necessarily. A correctly sized tank matched to actual peak usage often performs better than an oversized one that costs more to run continuously, since a larger tank still has to reheat that full volume repeatedly.

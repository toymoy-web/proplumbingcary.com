Toilet Running Constantly? Here's the Real Fix

Two parts cause almost every running toilet. Here's how to tell which one it is.

By the Cary Pro Plumbing team · September 19, 2026

Talk to a plumber
(919) 944-7281
Call Now

A toilet that runs constantly, or keeps refilling on its own every few minutes, is one of the most common plumbing complaints, and also one of the most consistently misdiagnosed by homeowners trying to fix it themselves without understanding what's actually happening inside the tank.

The Two Parts Responsible for Almost Every Case

A toilet tank has two main components that control water flow: the flapper, a rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that opens to flush and should close completely afterward, and the fill valve, which controls how the tank refills. A running toilet is caused by one of these two failing to do its job correctly, in the vast majority of cases.

How to Tell Which One Is the Problem

Remove the tank lid and watch what happens after a flush. If the flapper doesn't fully close, or you can see water slowly leaking past it into the bowl even when it looks closed, that's a flapper problem. If the water level keeps rising toward or into the overflow tube, or the fill valve keeps running even after the tank should be full, that's a fill valve problem.

A Simple Dye Test Confirms a Flapper Leak

Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank water without flushing, then wait 10 to 15 minutes. If color appears in the bowl without flushing, the flapper is letting water past it, confirming that's the source of the running sound even if it isn't obvious just from looking.

Why This Wastes More Water Than It Seems

A toilet that runs continuously, even quietly, can waste a significant amount of water over the course of a month, often showing up as an unexplained increase on a water bill before anyone notices the sound itself as a problem worth addressing.

When It's Worth Calling Rather Than DIY-ing It

Both the flapper and fill valve are inexpensive, widely available parts that many homeowners replace themselves successfully. It's worth calling for professional toilet repair if the replacement doesn't actually fix the running, since that usually means the flush valve seat itself is damaged, a less common but more involved repair, or if the toilet is old enough that finding compatible parts has become difficult.

What About a Toilet That Rocks Instead of Runs

A rocking toilet is a completely different problem, usually a failed wax ring seal or damaged flange underneath, and it's worth addressing sooner rather than later since it risks a floor leak. Don't confuse the two symptoms; they call for different fixes entirely.

Standards This Repair Follows

Toilet repair and replacement follows the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code, including flange height and water-use fixture requirements for any full toilet replacement, not just internal part swaps.

Cost Considerations

A flapper or fill valve replacement costs very little, whether done as a DIY repair or a professional visit, which is part of why this is one of the more affordable plumbing issues to address. A full toilet replacement, needed when the flush valve seat itself is damaged or parts are no longer available for an old model, costs considerably more but is still a modest project compared to most other plumbing repairs.

What A Slow Leak Sounds Like Versus A Full Run

A truly silent leak past the flapper can be harder to notice than a full continuous run, which is exactly why the dye test above is worth doing even when a toilet seems quiet, not just when it's audibly running.

A Note On Water-Efficient Models

Newer water-efficient toilets sometimes use proprietary internal components rather than universal parts, which is worth checking before assuming a generic hardware store part will fit.

Older Toilets And Part Availability

A toilet more than 20 years old sometimes uses a flapper or fill valve size that's harder to find at a standard hardware store, though universal-fit replacement parts cover most cases even for older models.

Multiple Toilets Running At Once

If more than one toilet in the home is running simultaneously, especially if this started suddenly, it can sometimes point to a water pressure or supply issue affecting the whole house rather than a coincidental double part failure, which is worth mentioning if you call for help.

Final Thought

A running toilet is one of the cheapest plumbing problems to actually fix, which makes letting it run for months the more expensive choice in the end.

A Quick Recap

Check the flapper and fill valve first, using a dye test to confirm a flapper leak specifically. Both parts are inexpensive to replace, and calling for professional help makes sense if a replacement doesn't actually resolve the running.

What Professional Repair Adds

Beyond replacing the obvious part, a professional visit checks the flush valve seat condition, confirms the water supply line and shutoff valve are in good shape, and tests the full flush cycle rather than just the immediate running symptom, catching a related issue before it becomes its own separate call.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a running toilet fix itself?

No, a worn flapper or fill valve only gets worse over time, not better.

How much does a running toilet actually cost in wasted water?

It varies by how severe the leak is and local water rates, but it's rarely negligible over a full billing cycle.

Is it normal for a toilet to run briefly after flushing?

Yes, a toilet refilling for 30 to 60 seconds after a flush is normal. Continuous running well beyond that isn't.

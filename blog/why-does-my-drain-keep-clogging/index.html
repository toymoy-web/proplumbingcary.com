Why Does My Drain Keep Clogging? 5 Real Causes

A clog that clears and returns is usually a different problem than a clog that clears and stays clear.

By the Cary Pro Plumbing team · September 14, 2026

Talk to a plumber
(919) 944-7281
Call Now

Snaking a drain clears whatever's immediately blocking it, which is often enough to solve a one-time clog for good. When the same drain clogs again within a few weeks, that pattern usually points to one of a handful of underlying causes, not bad luck.

The Code Standard Behind Drain Sizing

Drain and vent sizing follows the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code, which is part of why a line that was correctly sized decades ago can still struggle with modern low-flow fixtures using less water per flush than the code originally assumed.

1. Grease Buildup Coating the Pipe

In a kitchen line, grease doesn't clog a pipe all at once. It coats the inside walls a little at a time, narrowing the usable space until even small amounts of food debris catch and build up faster than before. Snaking punches through the immediate blockage but leaves the coating in place, which is why the same spot clogs again. Hydro jetting clears the coating itself rather than just the blockage sitting in it.

2. Root Intrusion in an Older Sewer Line

Tree roots find their way into a sewer line through small existing cracks or loose joints, then expand once inside, catching debris and slowing flow well before they fully block the pipe. This is more common in older neighborhoods with mature trees and clay or cast iron sewer laterals than in newer construction with PVC lines. A camera inspection during a sewer line visit is the only reliable way to confirm this versus assuming it based on the home's age.

3. A Bellied or Settled Pipe Section

Ground settling over time can create a low spot, or belly, in a section of pipe where water and debris collect instead of draining through. Snaking clears what's sitting in the belly temporarily, but the low spot itself doesn't go away, so debris collects there again.

4. Low-Flow Fixtures Not Moving Debris Effectively

Modern low-flow toilets and fixtures use less water per use, which is good for a water bill but sometimes isn't enough flow to fully carry debris through an older, larger-diameter drain line designed for higher-volume fixtures. This shows up more as a general slow-drain pattern than a hard clog, but it can present similarly.

5. A Partial Clog Further Down the Line

Sometimes the visible clog being snaked isn't the real blockage at all, just debris backed up against a partial clog further down the line. Clearing the visible portion provides temporary relief until debris builds up against the real obstruction again.

How to Actually Stop the Cycle

If a drain has clogged more than once in the same spot, a camera inspection before another round of snaking is usually the more cost-effective path. It identifies which of these five causes is actually responsible, rather than paying for repeated visits that only address the symptom each time.

How to Tell Which Cause Applies Without Guessing

The location and pattern of the clog narrow things down before a camera even goes in. A kitchen-only clog points toward grease. A clog affecting the main line or multiple fixtures at once points toward roots or a bellied section. A clog that's gradually gotten slower over months rather than happening suddenly fits the coating or low-flow patterns better than a sudden blockage.

What Hydro Jetting Actually Does Differently

A standard snake breaks through or pulls out whatever's directly blocking the line. Hydro jetting uses high-pressure water to scour the full interior surface of the pipe, removing grease coating, root intrusion, and built-up debris along the entire length treated, not just the immediate clog point. It's more thorough but also a different tool for a different kind of problem, which is why it isn't the default first step for every clog.

When a Clog Signals a Bigger Line Problem

A clog that keeps returning despite repeated professional snaking, not just a DIY attempt, is the clearer signal that something structural is going on rather than routine debris. At that point, a camera inspection stops being an upsell and starts being the more economical choice compared to paying for another temporary fix.

Why The Same Clog Location Matters More Than The Frequency

A clog that keeps happening in the exact same spot, rather than moving around to different fixtures, is a stronger signal of a structural cause like roots or a bellied section than a general pattern of slow drains across the house, which more often points to a house-wide issue like low water pressure or widespread mineral buildup instead.

What A Technician Actually Looks For On A Camera Inspection

A camera inspection isn't just about spotting an obvious blockage. It shows pipe material, joint condition, the presence and extent of root intrusion, any bellied sections, and the general condition of the pipe wall itself. That level of detail is what separates a real diagnosis from a guess based on the home's age or neighborhood.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal for a kitchen sink to clog more than other drains?

Yes, kitchen drains handle grease and food debris that bathroom and other drains don't, making them more prone to buildup over time.

Can chemical drain cleaners make a repeat clog worse?

They can, especially in older pipes, since repeated chemical use can degrade certain pipe materials and doesn't address root or structural causes at all.

How often should a camera inspection be done as maintenance?

For a home with no history of clogs, it's not typically needed. It becomes worthwhile once a repeat pattern shows up.

Is hydro jetting safe for every type of pipe?

It's safe for most modern piping, though a technician should confirm pipe condition first on older or already-compromised lines before using it, since a severely deteriorated pipe can react differently to high pressure.

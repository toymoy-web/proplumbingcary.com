Why Leaks Go Undetected For So Long

A leak inside a slab or behind a wall rarely announces itself the way a burst pipe does. Water can travel along a foundation footing or a joist bay for feet before it ever surfaces, so the wet spot on the floor or ceiling is often several feet from where the actual pipe failed.

In Cary specifically, this shows up two different ways depending on the neighborhood. Homes in Lochmere and MacGregor Downs are old enough that original copper supply lines are reaching the age where pinhole leaks start, while a newer build in Amberly or Copperleaf is more likely to be a fitting or connection that was never fully seated during construction.

The practical upshot is that guessing at a repair based on where the water surfaces usually means opening the wrong wall or digging in the wrong spot in the yard. Confirming the exact point first, before any cutting, is what keeps a leak repair from turning into a second, unnecessary repair on top of the first one.

Signs To Watch For

Signs You Have A Hidden Leak

Water Bill Jumped

A bill that's noticeably higher with no change in household water use is the most common first sign.

Meter Still Moving

If every fixture is off and the water meter is still turning, water is going somewhere in the line.

Warm Spot On The Floor

A warm patch on a slab floor usually points to a leak on the hot water line underneath it.

Mystery Damp Drywall

Soft or discolored drywall with no obvious source above it often means a wall leak, not roof damage.

Types Of Leaks

The Different Kinds Of Hidden Leaks We Trace

Slab Leaks

A supply line leaking under the concrete foundation itself, usually showing up as a warm floor spot or a climbing water bill with no visible cause.

Wall And Ceiling Leaks

Water traveling inside a wall cavity or above a ceiling before it ever becomes visible, often from a supply line, a shower pan, or a roof penetration.

Underground Yard Leaks

A leak in the line between the meter and the house, which can waste significant water without ever showing a wet spot on the surface.

Toilet And Fixture Leaks

A running toilet or a slow fixture leak that adds up on the water bill, sometimes mistaken for a bigger hidden leak until it's actually traced.

Each of these needs a different detection approach and a different repair, which is exactly why guessing based on symptoms alone, rather than actually tracing the leak, so often leads to the wrong fix.

How It Works

How Electronic Leak Detection Actually Works

Acoustic listening equipment picks up the specific sound water makes escaping a pressurized line, a sound that travels through pipe and soil differently depending on pressure, pipe material, and depth. A technician moves the equipment across the suspected area, narrowing in on where that sound is loudest.

For leaks that don't produce a clear acoustic signal, thermal imaging can spot temperature differences on a slab or wall surface caused by a hot water line leaking underneath, since the escaping hot water changes the surface temperature just enough to show up on a thermal camera even before any visible dampness appears.

In cases where both methods come back inconclusive, a non-toxic tracer gas can be introduced into the line and traced as it escapes through the leak point and rises through soil or a wall cavity, using a sensitive gas detector to pinpoint the exact spot from the surface.

DIY Monitoring Vs Professional Detection

What A Smart Water Monitor Can And Can't Tell You

Smart Water Monitor

  • Flags unusual water flow patterns
  • Can shut off water automatically
  • Does not locate the actual leak
  • Useful as an early warning, not a diagnosis

Professional Electronic Detection

  • Pinpoints the exact leak location
  • Confirms leak type and likely cause
  • Enables a targeted repair, not exploratory cutting
  • Combines acoustic, thermal, and tracer methods as needed

A smart water monitor is a genuinely useful early-warning tool, and pairing one with a home's plumbing is worth considering. It tells you that water is flowing somewhere it shouldn't be. It does not tell you where, which is the part electronic leak detection actually solves.

After The Leak Is Found

What Happens Once The Leak Is Confirmed

Once the leak's exact location is confirmed, the next step depends on what the equipment found. A single pinhole leak in an accessible section often means a targeted spot repair, cutting into only the confirmed area rather than opening up a larger section on a guess. A leak in a harder-to-reach spot, or one affecting a longer run of pipe already showing signs of age elsewhere, sometimes points toward rerouting that section entirely rather than repairing it in place.

Either way, the price is confirmed before any repair work begins, based on the actual confirmed location and scope, not an estimate made before the leak was even found. This is part of why detection and repair are priced as connected steps rather than two separate, unrelated visits.

Still Not Sure What's Wrong

"The Meter Keeps Running And I Don't Know Why"

This is the single most common thing we hear before a leak call: the meter keeps moving even with the water off inside, or the bill went up and there's no obvious cause. That combination almost always means an underground or slab leak rather than a fixture problem, and it's exactly what electronic leak tracing is built to solve without tearing up the yard or the floor to go looking for it.

While You Wait

What To Do Before We Arrive

Note The Meter Reading

Write down the current water meter reading so we can confirm how much water is being lost over time.

Avoid Digging Or Cutting

Don't dig in the yard or cut into drywall on your own. It can miss the actual leak and make tracing it harder.

Limit Water Use

Where possible, cut back on water use in the house until the leak is traced, especially if it may be on the hot side.

Leak repairs on the supply side follow the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code, the same code framework that governs plumbing work across the state.

FAQs

Common Questions

Do you have to cut into my wall or slab to find the leak?

No. Detection is done electronically first, and cutting only happens at the confirmed point once we know exactly where the leak is.

Can a leak really cause a high water bill with no visible water?

Yes, this is the most common way a slab or underground leak shows up. See our water damage restoration page if water has already reached flooring or drywall.

How is this different from a plumber just guessing and cutting drywall?

Electronic tracing narrows the leak to a specific point before any cutting, which is faster and less destructive than an exploratory approach.

Will you check the water heater and main line too while you're there?

Yes, if the source isn't obvious, we check the most common culprits first, including the water heater and visible supply lines, before moving to electronic tracing.

What if I've had more than one leak in the past couple of years?

That pattern often points past a single repair. See our whole-home repiping page for when it makes sense to replace the pipe material itself.

If the leak has already soaked flooring or drywall. If so, our Water Damage Restoration page covers that directly. A leaking tank shows up as water pooling near the unit. That's a job for Water Heater Repair instead.

If leaks keep happening in different spots rather than just once. In that case, take a look at our Whole-Home Repiping page.

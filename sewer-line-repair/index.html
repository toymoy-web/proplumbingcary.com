What Actually Fails In A Sewer Line

A sewer line rarely fails all at once. Root intrusion at a joint, a settling or bellied section, or a crack from ground shift all show up first as slow drains or recurring clogs before they become a full backup.

Older Cary neighborhoods with mature trees see more root intrusion into clay or cast iron laterals, while newer PVC lines are more likely to have a bellied section from soil settling during construction than an actual pipe failure.

Knowing which of these it actually is changes the fix entirely, which is why the camera inspection happens before any price is given, not after.

Signs To Watch For

Signs Of A Sewer Line Problem

Recurring Backups

A backup that clears and returns within weeks points to a structural line issue, not debris.

Sewage Smell In The Yard

An odor over a specific section of yard often marks where the line is compromised.

Multiple Drains Affected

Every fixture backing up at once means the main line, not an individual drain.

Soggy Or Sunken Yard Patch

A wet or sunken area along the line's path can indicate a leak or collapsed section underground.

Repair Method

Trenchless Or Traditional Dig

Traditional Excavation

  • Full yard disruption along the line
  • Necessary for severely collapsed sections
  • Typically longer completion time
  • Often lower cost for short runs

Trenchless Repair

  • Minimal yard disruption
  • Works well for cracks and root intrusion
  • Faster completion in most cases
  • Requires the line to still hold its shape

If A Backup Is Active

What To Do Right Now

Stop Using Water

Avoid running water or flushing toilets until the backup clears, since it can push sewage further into the home.

Don't Attempt To Dig

Avoid digging near the suspected line yourself. It can miss the actual damage and complicate the real repair.

Note Which Fixtures Are Affected

Which drains are backing up helps narrow down whether the issue is the main line or a single branch.

Sewer line repair follows the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code for slope, material, and connection requirements.

FAQs

Common Questions

Do I need a full line replacement or just a spot repair?

That depends entirely on what the camera shows. A single damaged joint is usually a spot repair, not a full replacement.

Will you have to dig up my whole yard?

Not necessarily. Trenchless repair options avoid that for many types of damage, though a badly collapsed section may still need excavation.

Is this different from a simple drain clog?

Yes. See our drain cleaning page if snaking alone has been enough in the past, since that suggests debris rather than a structural line issue.

Can tree roots really crack a sewer line?

Roots typically enter through an existing small crack or joint gap rather than cracking solid pipe, then expand the damage over time.

When Replacement Beats Another Repair

Most tank water heaters are built to last 10 to 15 years. Past that point, the tank itself, not just the parts inside it, is the thing wearing out, and no repair fixes a tank that's corroding from the inside.

This comes up often in Cary's older sections like Lochmere and MacGregor Downs, where original units from the 1980s and 90s building boom are now well past that window. Newer Amberly and Copperleaf homes see fewer replacement calls simply because the units haven't aged into that range yet.

The decision usually comes down to a simple comparison: the cost of one more repair against the cost of a new unit that won't need another repair call for a decade or more. We lay out both numbers before recommending either one, since a unit that's still under 8 years old is often still worth repairing even if this isn't its first service call.

Sizing matters as much as the unit type. A tank or tankless system sized for a smaller household will run out of hot water or short-cycle in a house with more bathrooms, so we size the replacement to the home rather than swapping in a like-for-like unit by default.

Signs To Watch For

Signs It's Time To Replace, Not Repair

Unit Over 10 Years Old

Past this age, tank failure becomes a matter of when, not if.

Rust In The Water

Rust means the tank lining has broken down internally, which a repair can't reverse.

Repeat Repairs

A second or third repair in a short window usually costs more over time than replacing.

Rising Energy Bills

Older units lose efficiency as sediment builds up inside the tank, so a newer, properly sized unit often pays part of its own cost back over time.

Tank Vs Tankless

Which Fits Your Home

Traditional Tank

  • Lower upfront cost
  • Simpler installation
  • Limited hot water at once
  • Typical 10-15 year lifespan

Tankless

  • Continuous hot water on demand
  • Lower ongoing energy use
  • Compact wall-mounted unit
  • Typical 20+ year lifespan

Before We Arrive

How To Get Ready For Installation

Clear The Area

Clear a path to the unit so it can be removed and the new one carried in without obstruction.

Know Your Fuel Type

Have your gas or electric type on hand if known. It affects which replacement units are a direct fit.

Note Any Existing Leaks

Mention any moisture you've already noticed around the base so we check for damage to the surrounding area.

New installations follow the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted by the North Carolina State Building Code, including venting and clearance requirements that don't apply to a straightforward repair.
